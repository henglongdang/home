01Profile

Practical problems, examined as systems

Most of my work starts with a practical problem and ends in the data.

A household juggling several finance apps. A 0.5% tax line on a closing statement. In each case the visible issue sits on top of interacting parts—software, policy, habits, markets—and the work is to measure them: survey design and inferential statistics for the finance study, a weighted composite index and transaction-cost modeling for housing, coded qualitative data where numbers alone would mislead. Then I turn the analysis into something people can use: a prototype, a calculator, a set of measures.

Alongside that, I write about what happens to human judgment when software suggests, ranks, and decides: two books so far, and an ongoing series of essays.

02Selected work

Research and projects

The two projects with the most evidence behind them, then two organizations I have helped run.

Peer-reviewed paper · Survey analytics and HCI

Revolutionizing Household Finance: A User-Centered Platform for Holistic Financial Management

Dang, H. & Ding, F. · HCI International 2025 – Late Breaking Papers, LNCS 16342, pp. 22–40 · Springer, 2026

Households track budgets, bills, investments, and forecasts in separate apps. A 342-person survey showed that the more tools people juggle, the more cognitive fatigue they report (r = 0.58, p < .001); a factor analysis reduced fourteen pain points to three; and an integrated prototype tested with 16 users scored 84.2 on the System Usability Scale.

Independent research · Housing policy · Jan–Jun 2026

Housing Friction Index

Bellevue, Washington · weighted composite index, cost modeling, coded interviews and fieldwork

Bellevue’s 0.5% local real estate excise tax adds $7,000 to a $1.4 million sale. I used that line item as an entry point to model how taxes, zoning, parking, permitting, mortgage rates, and supply accumulate into affordability pressure—and found that, on its own, the tax is one of the smaller forces.
