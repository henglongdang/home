About the book

The book is built around an idea I call the Great Outsourcing: the point at which people and organizations hand not only tasks but judgment to automated systems. It is not an argument against technology. It is an argument about where decisions actually get made once software is involved, and about the design choices that decide whether a person remains the one doing the thinking.

It challenges the story of “automated perfection” and asks questions that leaders, designers, and citizens increasingly have to answer, whether or not they notice they are answering them.

How assistance becomes authority

A recurring pattern in the book: systems introduced to help people decide gradually take over the deciding. Select a step to see how the book describes it.

Central questions

  1. Is “assistance” quietly becoming authority?

    How nudges, default settings, and ranked lists steer professional choices before a human ever clicks.

  2. Is the “human in the loop” a myth?

    Why the final signature on a machine-generated report does not make you the final thinker.

  3. What happens when judgment is not practiced?

    Decision atrophy and the fate of professional expertise.

  4. Should good systems be harder to agree with?

    The case for friction-heavy designs that invite disagreement rather than compliance.

How it was written

The book was written in partnership with AI tools, and says so. Its description sets out the division of labour: the machine contributed data synthesis and linguistic articulation; I contributed the moral judgment, lived experience, and the qualitative framework. Given the subject, I think readers should know that, and can judge for themselves whether the book makes its own case—that a person steering firmly can use these systems without surrendering authorship.

Who it is for

Leaders weighing the ethics of AI adoption, designers building the next generation of interfaces, and anyone uneasy about the erosion of choice in a world increasingly mediated by systems. It is dense by design. Its last instruction to the reader: stop asking what machines can do, and start asking what they cause us to stop doing.

It follows Echoes in the Machine, which looked at what AI does to knowledge; this book turns to decisions. The essays continue both lines of thought.
