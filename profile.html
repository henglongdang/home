In brief

I study statistics and data science at UCLA. Statistics has left me with a lasting suspicion of single numbers. A satisfaction score, a tax rate, a member count: each means something only next to its scale, its sample, and what it leaves out.

My work moves between three settings that turn out to have a lot in common. In research, I have used a 342-person survey, ANOVA, correlation, and factor analysis to study how households manage money across fragmented apps, and built a weighted cost index to study how small housing costs stack into large ones. In writing, I have spent two books on what happens to human judgment once software starts to suggest, rank, and decide. In operations, I have built a community organization from nothing, coordinated a design studio across the Pacific, and now help people at UCLA Anderson when their technology stops cooperating.

The common thread is that the visible problem is rarely the whole problem.

What I work on

Statistics and data science

Survey design, descriptive and inferential statistics, factor analysis, composite indices, and cost modeling, applied to real questions and reported with their limits. The methods page lists each method with the result it produced.

Business analytics

Turning data into decisions: feature priorities set from user ratings and a business model for the FamilyPulse product concept; municipal revenue weighed against market effects in the housing study; goals tied to budget targets on the school district coalition.

Decisions inside software

How interface structure shapes what people can understand and do. My HCII 2025 paper showed that the number of finance tools people juggle correlates with cognitive fatigue (r = 0.58) and tested a unified alternative with 16 users.

Systems problems in civic life

Policy as people experience it. The Housing Friction Index traces how taxes, zoning, parking, and rates accumulate; on the school district coalition I argued for measures that capture what numbers miss.

Judgment and AI

Two books—Echoes in the Machine on knowledge and Human-Centered, Sustainable, and Socially-Responsible Innovation on decisions—and an ongoing series of essays.

How I work

  1. Start from a concrete observation

    A tax line on my own closing statement started the housing project; the everyday experience of juggling separate finance apps started the household finance paper. Abstract questions come later.

  2. Find the layers underneath

    Ask what the visible issue sits on. In technology support that is account, device, network, service; in housing it is tax, zoning, financing, supply.

  3. Measure, then test

    Describe the data first, then test the claim: an ANOVA for group differences, a correlation for association, a factor analysis to find structure. Pair numbers with interviews or fieldwork, because each covers what the other cannot.

  4. Say what the evidence cannot show

    A modeled correlation is not a finding; seven interviews are not a population. I would rather state the limit than have a reader discover it.

  5. Make the result usable

    A prototype, a calculator, a course, a clear explanation at a help desk. Analysis that nobody can act on is unfinished.

Running things

I founded Synaperge in August 2023 and led it until September 2026. It grew to more than 60 active members, ran discussion-led seminars, a Problem Lab, a free course, and showcases, and by its second year had a rotating board so that it did not depend on me. Since 2024 I have handled strategic operations at DFDraw Lab, a design studio whose work spans the United States and China. Since October 2026 I have worked as a technology consultant at UCLA Anderson.

These roles taught me different versions of the same lesson: structure is what lets other people act without asking you first. A clear handoff note, a well-defined project owner, and a support case written up properly all do the same job.
