Revolutionizing Household Finance: A User-Centered Platform for Holistic Financial Management

A mixed-methods study of why household money management is spread across disconnected apps, and a prototype that brings budgeting, bills, accounts, forecasting, and family planning into one place.

Summary

Most households do not manage money in one place. A budget lives in one app, bills in another, bank balances in a third, and the conversation about next month’s expenses happens in a group chat or a spreadsheet. Each tool works; the whole does not.

This paper asks what people actually need from a household finance tool and whether a single, shared platform serves them better than a collection of separate ones. We combined a survey of 342 people with 35 interviews, used the findings to design a prototype that integrates the main financial tasks and adds shared planning for family members, and refined it through repeated usability testing.

Abstract

The increasing complexity of household financial management has been exacerbated by the fragmented architecture of existing digital financial tools. Although discrete applications support budgeting, expenditure tracking, forecasting, and account oversight, a critical gap remains: the absence of a unified platform that facilitates integrated financial planning and collaborative familial governance. This study introduces a comprehensive digital solution aimed at remedying this deficiency through a consolidated user interface augmented by predictive analytics.

Consequently, this paper delineates and assesses a holistic platform that directly ameliorates the functional fragmentation. Empirical inquiries, encompassing quantitative (N = 342 survey) and qualitative (N = 35 interviews) methodologies, discerned salient disutility points, revealing significant cognitive burden and a low satisfaction (2.7/5) with extant applications. An iterative prototype developmental trajectory, progressing from low-fidelity schematics to a high-fidelity interactive simulacrum, was informed by key dissatisfactions identified in the initial user research and insights from iterative usability validation cycles. Subsequent comparative performance assessment corroborated the platform’s integrative schema, with a mean satisfaction of the proposed prototype of 84.2 out of 100 (SD = 7.9). Through synthesis of rigorous empirical methodologies with sophisticated algorithmic prognostications and resilient cooperative functionalities, this platform furnishes a pragmatic resolution, establishing a scalable archetypal framework for holistic, human-centered financial applications, augmenting the state of the art of financial management applications.

The problem: one household, many tools

Fragmentation is not only an inconvenience. When each tool holds part of the picture, the work of connecting them moves into people’s heads: remembering which app shows what, re-entering the same figures, reconciling a forecast against spending that lives elsewhere, and relaying all of it to the other people in the household. The paper describes this as cognitive burden, and the survey and interviews found it to be significant.

Research questions

The study was organized around three questions, each broken into more specific ones. They are transcribed from the original project proposal.

  1. How do users’ financial management needs and preferences vary based on demographic factors?

    • Which financial management tasks do users find most challenging, and what obstacles hinder effective use of family finance apps?

  2. What features do users find most valuable in a family finance app?

    • How do users currently manage their budgets and expenses, and what tools or methods do they use?
    • How effectively are real-time notifications influencing users’ financial behaviors and decision-making?
    • How do users currently track their spending?

  3. What factors influence users’ trust in a family finance app?

    • What privacy and security concerns do users have when using family finance apps and sharing financial information?

The proposed platform

The prototype—developed as a product concept called FamilyPulse—answers the fragmentation problem with three design commitments. These are design proposals, not findings.

  1. One surface

    A single dashboard that combines budgeting, transaction tracking, shared household goals, and account visibility, so no figure has to be carried between apps.

  2. Look ahead

    Predictive insights that flag upcoming expenses and potential shortfalls, connected to the same records as day-to-day spending.

  3. Plan together

    Shared categories, role-aware access, and coordinated planning routines so that a household can manage money as a group without every member seeing everything.

My role

I was first and corresponding author. I framed the research problem, designed the empirical study, synthesized the survey and interview findings, and translated them into product requirements. I defined the integrated interaction model and the collaborative household and forecasting workflows, led the prototype through its low- and high-fidelity iterations, and presented the paper at HCII 2025.

Reflection

The study shows that people find a unified, shared tool more satisfying to use than the patchwork they currently rely on. It does not yet show that such a tool changes how households actually manage money. The evaluation measured satisfaction with a prototype; a natural next step would be a longitudinal study of a working version, tracking whether shared visibility changes planning behaviour, disagreements, or missed payments over months rather than minutes.

The trust question also deserves more attention than a prototype can give it. Asking a family to put every account in one place concentrates risk as well as convenience, and the privacy concerns raised in research question 3 would need to be tested against a real data model, not a mock-up.
