Revolutionizing Household Finance: A User-Centered Platform for Holistic Financial Management

A sequential mixed-methods study of why household money management is spread across disconnected apps—quantified with survey statistics and factor analysis—and an integrated prototype evaluated with standard usability metrics.

Summary

Most households do not manage money in one place. A budget lives in one app, bills in another, investments in a third, and coordination happens in a group chat or a spreadsheet. The paper measures what that fragmentation costs people and tests whether a single, shared platform does better.

The study ran in three phases: a 342-person survey analysed with descriptive and inferential statistics, 35 follow-up interviews with screen-shared observation, and an iterative prototype that ended in a task-based evaluation with 16 participants. The central quantitative result is a strong correlation between the number of tools people juggle and the cognitive fatigue they report (r = 0.58, p < .001).

  • 331complete survey responses (of 342; 96.8%)

  • 71.2%of pain-point variance explained by three factors

Two different instruments. Satisfaction with existing apps (2.7 out of 5) is a Likert item from the survey. The prototype’s 84.2 is a System Usability Scale score from the evaluation. They are not a before-and-after comparison of one measure. All figures on this page come from the published paper.

Abstract

The increasing complexity of household financial management has been exacerbated by the fragmented architecture of existing digital financial tools. Although discrete applications support budgeting, expenditure tracking, forecasting, and account oversight, a critical gap remains: the absence of a unified platform that facilitates integrated financial planning and collaborative familial governance. This study introduces a comprehensive digital solution aimed at remedying this deficiency through a consolidated user interface augmented by predictive analytics.

Consequently, this paper delineates and assesses a holistic platform that directly ameliorates the functional fragmentation. Empirical inquiries, encompassing quantitative (N = 342 survey) and qualitative (N = 35 interviews) methodologies, discerned salient disutility points, revealing significant cognitive burden and a low satisfaction (2.7/5) with extant applications. An iterative prototype developmental trajectory, progressing from low-fidelity schematics to a high-fidelity interactive simulacrum, was informed by key dissatisfactions identified in the initial user research and insights from iterative usability validation cycles. Subsequent comparative performance assessment corroborated the platform’s integrative schema, with a mean satisfaction of the proposed prototype of 84.2 out of 100 (SD = 7.9). Through synthesis of rigorous empirical methodologies with sophisticated algorithmic prognostications and resilient cooperative functionalities, this platform furnishes a pragmatic resolution, establishing a scalable archetypal framework for holistic, human-centered financial applications, augmenting the state of the art of financial management applications.

Keywords: Predictive Analytics, Fintech, Human-Centered Design, Financial Management, Functional Fragmentation.

The problem: functional fragmentation

The paper calls it functional fragmentation: essential tasks—budgeting, expense tracking, forecasting, collaboration—spread across systems that do not talk to each other. A household might track spending in Mint, budget in YNAB, follow investments in Robinhood, and get bill reminders from Prism. Data silos and incompatible APIs push the reconciliation work back onto people, by hand.

The literature review adds two gaps. A review of 170 financial tools found that most specialize narrowly and omit planning and shared use; and fewer than one in four budgeting apps reviewed supported forward-looking planning. Most tools look backward, not ahead.

Research questions

Transcribed from the original project proposal.

  1. How do users’ financial management needs and preferences vary based on demographic factors?

    • Which financial management tasks do users find most challenging, and what obstacles hinder effective use of family finance apps?

  2. What features do users find most valuable in a family finance app?

    • How do users currently manage their budgets and expenses, and what tools or methods do they use?
    • How effectively are real-time notifications influencing users’ financial behaviors and decision-making?
    • How do users currently track their spending?

  3. What factors influence users’ trust in a family finance app?

    • What privacy and security concerns do users have when using family finance apps and sharing financial information?

Study design

A sequential exploratory mixed-methods design in three phases: empirical user inquiry, iterative system design, and comparative usability benchmarking. Each phase fed the next. Select a stage for details.

Sample

Non-probability voluntary sampling, aimed at varied financial identities and household structures. Participants had to be over 18, use at least one digital finance tool regularly, and take part in financial decisions alone or with others. Respondents came from North America, Europe, Southeast Asia, East Asia, and South America.

  • 52.6%millennials; 36.7% Gen Z
  • 41.2%some income from self-employment or freelancing
  • 28.9%manage a shared budget (couples, roommates, families)
  • 17.8%manage money on behalf of others

Statistical analysis

The 342 responses were screened for incomplete answers, meaningless inputs, and missing values, leaving 331 for analysis. Descriptive statistics came first, then three inferential steps.

1. Who is least satisfied: one-way ANOVA

Mean satisfaction with current tools was 2.7 out of 5 (SD = 1.1). It was lowest for freelancers and couples who manage money together. A one-way ANOVA found a statistically significant difference in satisfaction across income groups, F(2, 328) = 5.84, p < .01, suggesting that volatile income makes inadequate tools feel worse.

2. Does more tools mean more fatigue: Pearson correlation

The number of tools a respondent used correlated strongly and positively with reported cognitive fatigue, r = 0.58, p < .001. This is the study’s most direct quantitative support for the claim that fragmentation itself creates burden. Being correlational, it does not on its own establish that adding tools causes fatigue.

3. What the pain points have in common: exploratory factor analysis

Fourteen pain-related scale items were analysed with principal axis factoring and Promax rotation. Sampling adequacy was good (Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin = 0.84) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ²(91) = 973.24, p < .001). Three factors explained 71.2% of the variance.

4. What people want: feature ratings

On 5-point scales, the three most valued features were the ability to simulate future finances, role-based permissions for shared money, and a consolidated dashboard. The narrow standard deviations show broad agreement.

Qualitative findings

Workarounds

People bridged their tools by hand: regular CSV exports, copy-pasting balances, screenshots shared with partners, offline ledgers while travelling, and periodic manual audits. One participant navigated five nested menus in Mint just to find a recurring bill.

Predictive mental models

Users expected tools to look ahead, describing what they wanted as a “financial GPS,” a “weather forecast,” or “look-ahead budgeting.” Freelancers in particular could not see income gaps over multi-week spans.

Collaboration happens outside the tools

Couples fell back on weekly sync-ups and shared documents; one participant toggled between a spreadsheet and WhatsApp to confirm shared grocery payments.

Single-user assumptions

Tools assumed one user with one income and ignored irregular expenses, leaving out co-parents, roommates, gig workers, and people managing money for dependents.

Evaluation

Sixteen participants (8 women, 8 men; aged 23–56, M = 35.1, SD = 6.4) completed 45-minute remote sessions with think-aloud. Eight had used YNAB or PocketSmith before. Task order was counterbalanced to avoid learning effects.

  1. Shared budget

    Create a household budget with categories, contributors, and thresholds.

  2. Income shock

    Simulate a 30% income drop and find the first month in deficit.

  3. Shared expense

    Log an expense and tag another user in a comment.

  4. Delay a payment

    See the forecast impact of paying a bill two weeks late.

  5. Resolve a conflict

    Fix a conflicting entry using the activity feed and edit history.

  • 94%average task completion
  • 3.4 minmean time per task (SD 1.1)
  • 0.47errors per task, mostly permission settings
  • 84.2System Usability Scale (SD 7.9)

What worked

The scenario planner was called “confidence boosting” and “more transparent than spreadsheets”; the ledger was praised for its audit trail. Participants said it replaced what they had been stitching together from spreadsheets and messaging threads.

What it taught

Forecasting needs progressive disclosure for people new to modelling income volatility. Households prefer asynchronous coordination—comments, edit rights, activity logs—over real-time co-editing. Some wanted weekly as well as monthly views, and three asked for Venmo or Zelle integration.

Limits and next steps

  • Ecological validity. The evaluation ran remotely with simulated data; bank integrations (Plaid, Yodlee) were mocked.
  • Sample. Voluntary, non-probability sampling that skewed toward digitally literate users, so accessibility needs of less tech-fluent people may be under-represented.
  • Scope of the prototype. No machine-learning models for personalized forecasting or anomaly detection yet; scalability and mobile-first deployment untested.
  • Next. A working system with live financial APIs and better forecasting models, followed by a longitudinal field study of goal attainment, collaboration, and sustained use.
