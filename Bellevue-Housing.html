From a closing estimate to a question

In early 2026 I was preparing to sell a home in Bellevue, Washington, and read the seller’s cost sheet more carefully than I ever had. Commissions, title, escrow, repairs, and concessions all cut into the proceeds. One line stood out because it depended on where the house was: a local real estate excise tax (REET) of 0.5% of the sale price.

Half a percent sounds negligible. At Bellevue prices it is not: on a $1.4 million sale it is $7,000, before anything else. That raised a broader question. At what point do accumulated housing-market costs—none decisive on its own—start to change what people do?

  • 0.5%Bellevue’s local REET, on top of the state tax
  • $7,000local REET on a $1.4 million sale

Disclosure. The project began with my own transaction. I treat that as a disclosure, not as evidence: claims in the study rest on public sources, coded interviews, field observations, and transparent arithmetic.

One thesis, five questions

Primary hypothesis. When prices and borrowing costs are already high, added transaction frictions can influence seller behaviour, buyer participation, and mobility, even when no single cost explains affordability by itself. The local REET is framed as one measurable friction—not as the cause of unaffordability.

  1. Does Bellevue’s local REET create additional transaction friction?

    Method: calculate the local tax burden across sale-price bands and compare with a no-local-REET scenario.

  2. Do sellers incorporate REET costs into listing prices?

    Method: code agent interviews for net-proceeds sensitivity, resistance to concessions, and pricing language.

  3. Does that behaviour affect housing affordability?

    Method: compare buyer topic mentions, modeled payment pressure, and estimated cost pass-through.

  4. How important is REET revenue for Bellevue’s infrastructure?

    Method: review capital-facility and budget materials to separate the revenue’s purpose from its market effect.

  5. Could alternative structures raise similar revenue?

    Method: scenario assumptions for thresholds, exemptions, credits, and broader funding mixes.

The Housing Friction Model

The model treats affordability as the combined result of several constraints rather than a single variable. Transaction taxes are one channel among six. Around all of them sit the behavioural responses that turn costs into outcomes: households delay, reprice, concede, or stay put. The Friction Score

To compare unlike forces side by side, the project defines a composite index. Each component is normalized to a 0–100 scale, then weighted:

Friction Score = 0.25 × REET burden percentile + 0.20 × zoning constraint + 0.15 × parking cost + 0.15 × permit delay + 0.15 × mortgage-rate exposure + 0.10 × street-width walkability penalty

REET gets the largest single weight because it is where the project started, but no component can dominate the total. Scores above 70 indicate several channels active at once; 40–70, mixed friction; below 40, lower exposure. The index is meant to be read comparatively, not as a forecast.

What practitioners and buyers said

Two sources of first-hand evidence tested whether the tax shows up in real decisions. In structured interviews, seven Eastside real estate professionals—listing and buyer specialists, a broker, a new-construction specialist, an investor advisor, and a market analyst—were asked about transaction costs, buyer pressure, and seller behaviour. Each topic was coded for concern intensity on a 1–5 scale.

At an open house I hosted, I logged what visitor groups raised unprompted, counting each topic once per group. Buyers began with the monthly payment and rates, not the house.

Method and timeline

The method was deliberately mixed: documents for legal structure, interviews for market behaviour, fieldwork for buyer language, and data review for economic context.

  1. Documents

    State REET statutes, Bellevue budget and capital materials, zoning concepts, parking-cost assumptions, housing-strategy records.

  2. Interviews

    Seven Eastside professionals; names, offices, and client details removed; responses coded by topic and intensity.

  3. Fieldwork

    Open-house observation of buyer concerns, plus street-width and pedestrian-comfort notes.

  4. Policy analysis

    Revenue purpose compared with market pressure, housing-production incentives, and liquidity.

  5. Synthesis

    Public recommendations, a research exhibit, and a roadmap for statistical testing.

Findings

The tax is not the main driver

Considered on its own, Bellevue’s REET is unlikely to be a primary cause of unaffordability. Regional supply constraints, population growth, mortgage rates, and land scarcity carry far more weight.

Small costs still matter in a stack

Because housing markets are shaped by many frictions at once, modest transaction costs can still contribute to reduced mobility and higher barriers to participation when they combine with existing pressure.

Policy is evaluated too narrowly

Measures meant to raise revenue, regulate development, fund infrastructure, or protect neighbourhood character interact, and can create cumulative burdens no single measure was designed to impose.

Reform, not repeal

The practical recommendations: publish plain-language seller guidance with dollar examples; study how REET revenue behaves across market cycles; and model alternatives—thresholds, exemptions, credits, parking reform—against the capital plan.

Limits, stated plainly

  • No control group yet. A valid test would compare Bellevue with a matched high-income ZIP code in a municipality without a local REET, over the same months. Until that comparison exists, results are exploratory.
  • Modeled, not measured, associations. The full exhibit includes a modeled correlation of 0.39 between REET exposure and days on market. It is a hypothesis map for future work, not evidence that the tax lengthens listings.
  • Small samples. Seven interviews and one set of open houses can reveal recurring language; they cannot estimate how a population thinks.
  • Confidence varies. Strongest for the transaction arithmetic and source documentation, moderate for interview patterns, low for predictions about future market outcomes.

Abstract and sources

Full abstract

Housing affordability is frequently discussed through the lens of home prices, interest rates, and housing supply. Far less attention is given to the cumulative effect of transaction frictions: the taxes, fees, regulations, procedural requirements, and market barriers that influence the cost and ease of housing mobility.

This study investigates whether seemingly modest transaction-related costs can collectively shape housing affordability, market participation, and residential mobility in high-cost urban environments.

The research originated from a practical observation during the sale of a residential property in Bellevue, Washington. While reviewing closing documents, a municipal Real Estate Excise Tax (REET) of 0.5% emerged as a notable transaction cost. Although relatively small when viewed independently, the tax raised a broader question: at what point do accumulated housing-market frictions begin to influence homeowner behavior, transaction decisions, and overall housing affordability?

To address this question, the study expands beyond the Bellevue REET itself and develops a broader analytical framework termed the Housing Friction Model. This framework conceptualizes housing affordability as the product of interacting constraints rather than a single market variable.

Under this model, transaction taxes represent only one component within a larger ecosystem that includes zoning restrictions, parking requirements, permitting processes, mortgage rates, land-use regulations, development costs, and behavioral responses by buyers and sellers.

Using Bellevue as a case study, the project combines policy analysis, municipal financial data, housing-market indicators, public records, stakeholder interviews, open-house observations, and comparative research from peer municipalities. The investigation evaluates how transaction costs influence market outcomes and explores whether these costs operate independently or amplify the effects of other affordability pressures.

Particular attention is given to the distinction between direct financial burdens and indirect behavioral consequences, including reduced mobility, delayed transactions, pricing adjustments, and diminished market flexibility.

The analysis finds that the Bellevue REET is unlikely to be a primary driver of housing unaffordability when considered in isolation. Larger structural forces, including regional housing supply constraints, population growth, mortgage interest rates, and land scarcity, exert substantially greater influence on overall housing costs.

However, the study suggests that transaction-related costs remain relevant because housing markets are shaped by the accumulation of numerous frictions rather than a single dominant factor. In this context, even relatively modest transaction costs may contribute to broader patterns of reduced mobility and increased barriers to market participation when combined with other existing constraints.

The research further demonstrates that housing policy is often evaluated through isolated interventions despite operating within highly interconnected systems. Policies intended to generate municipal revenue, regulate development, improve infrastructure, or preserve neighborhood character frequently interact in ways that create unintended cumulative burdens.

As a result, affordability outcomes may emerge not from any single regulation, tax, or market condition, but from the collective weight of multiple overlapping constraints.

The central thesis of this project is that housing affordability should be understood as a systems problem rather than a single-policy problem. Effective housing policy requires evaluating the cumulative impact of housing-market frictions and recognizing how small costs can interact with larger structural forces. The Bellevue REET serves as a representative case through which broader questions of housing mobility, market efficiency, and affordability can be examined.

Ultimately, this study argues that policymakers should move beyond evaluating individual housing policies in isolation and instead adopt a friction-based framework that measures the combined effects of taxes, regulations, procedural requirements, and market barriers. Such an approach may provide a more accurate understanding of affordability challenges and support the development of policies that preserve public objectives while minimizing unintended obstacles to housing access and mobility.
