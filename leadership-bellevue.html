The charge

Bellevue School District’s strategic plan was co-created in 2023–24 through empathy interviews and community design sessions. That process produced commitments—Belong, Learn, Lead—but not yet a way to measure progress against them. The coalition’s job in 2024–25 was exactly that: to develop the five-year goals, measures, and targets, working alongside the district’s design teams.

Members were chosen three ways to widen who was in the room: by application with random selection within groups, by appointment (including two board directors), and by sortition—direct outreach to people who might not apply through the usual route.

The problem the plan set out to address

The 2023–24 design process ended in a problem statement: each member of the district’s diverse community wants to feel heard, valued, and affirmed, but there was not yet an organizational culture that helps each person experience belonging and support. It named four root causes:

  • The Bellevue reputation: extraordinary aggregate outcomes, but intense stratification.
  • Efficiency and inequity: a system not designed to respond to diverse needs.
  • Unresponsive systems: not organized to partner with students, families, and educators.
  • Transactional relationships: centred on compliance rather than empowerment, trust, and agency.

The first root cause is itself a statistical point: strong averages can hide wide gaps between groups, which is why the published goals are disaggregated by student group.

The plan’s framework

The district organizes the plan around three commitments resting on a foundation of sustainable systems.

The measurement blend

The plan’s central measurement decision is that no goal is tracked by numbers alone. Each pairs three kinds of evidence:

Quantitative

What can be counted and compared statistically over time—assessment scores, attendance rates, fund balance—disaggregated by student group. Annual progress is reported on these.

Qualitative

What people say and how they describe it: focus groups, interviews, open responses. It supplies the “why” behind a number.

Experiential

What people encounter in real time: empathy interviews, user journeys, stories. It shows how a system feels from the student’s or family’s side.

Why it matters

A single metric invites optimizing the metric. Pairing a rate with the reasons behind it and the experience of it makes it harder to hit a target while missing the point.

The first annual plan (2025–26) applies the blend to three focus goals. Select one.

Five-year targets

The published plan sets two headline targets for 2030. Chronic absenteeism—missing more than 10% of the school year—should fall from 25% to its pre-pandemic level of 20% districtwide, with no student group above 30%; the stretch goal is to halve it. Literacy and math proficiency should rise for all students, by at least 15 percentage points for historically underserved groups (stretch: 25 points).

Questions the coalition raised about measurement

In a “See, Think, Wonder” review of the district’s existing measures, members recorded concerns that read like a methods checklist:

  • More than one measure should be used for each area.
  • Surveys can produce false answers; how do we make them less threatening, and check whether what people say matches what they do?
  • Low attendance does not equal a low sense of belonging.
  • Standardized tests may not reflect every culture.
  • How do we collect data accurately across different populations, and how often should it be collected?
  • Accountability has to stay connected to the stories: if a story does not change after an action, why not?

Paraphrased from the coalition’s group notes; not attributed to individual members.

My contribution

I was one of 41 members, and the plan belongs to the district. My input concentrated on two things.

  • Qualitative measures alongside numbers. Where goals were set as quantitative targets, I argued for measures that capture what the numbers miss—belonging, participation, access, and the barriers students themselves report.
  • Student voice. Advising on ways for students to have a larger and more direct role in decisions that affect them, and contributing equity-focused input to the five-year strategies.

It was the same problem I work on in research—choosing measures that are valid for the thing you care about—at the scale of a public institution working under budget constraints and staffing reductions.
