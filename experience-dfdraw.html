The studio

DFDraw Lab is a small, independent design studio. Its work covers brand identity, visual systems, corporate communications such as investor presentations and publications, and digital products—websites and apps—along with book publishing, custom product development, and interior design. Clients and collaborators are in both the United States and China.

That combination creates the operational problem I work on. A studio this size has no layer of managers to absorb coordination, the work spans very different disciplines, and the two halves of the team are rarely awake at the same time. Good ideas are not scarce; getting them through planning, handoffs, and review without losing a day at each step is.

My role

  • Structural planning and internal governance: how work is organized, who decides what, and how decisions are recorded.
  • Coordination across disciplines: keeping design, product, and publishing work aligned when they draw on the same people.
  • Long-range initiatives: turning studio goals into plans with owners and sequence, and following them through implementation.
  • Process reform: changing workflows so the studio could take on more work and add people without coordination breaking down.

The operating loop

Most of the job is a cycle: direction becomes a plan, the plan becomes coordinated work, work is delivered and reviewed, and the review changes the next round of direction. Select a stage to see what my part in it is.

Working across fifteen or sixteen hours

China Standard Time is eight hours ahead of UTC all year. US Pacific Time is seven or eight hours behind, depending on daylight saving. So the gap is 15 hours for most of the year and 16 in winter—and ordinary office hours barely meet. Switch between the two periods below.

The practical consequence is that almost nothing can be settled in a quick conversation. A question asked mid-morning in the US arrives in China after midnight; the answer comes back the next US evening. Work has to be handed over in writing, complete enough that the other side can act on it without asking, and planning has to anticipate which decisions will be needed before the overlap window closes.
